## How a card reads

- **ID.** It stays the same across levels and rounds, and a retake compares answers by it.
- **Asked at.** Starter (beginner), Building (intermediate) or Researching (advanced). A level
  with no wording of its own uses the next simpler one.
- **Why.** One plain sentence said before the *Ask*, at every level: why the question is asked,
  so it makes sense to someone who has none of the asker's context. It is never written.
- **Ask.** The question, as worded at that level: one thing to answer. Q3, Q6, Q8 and Q14 at
  Starter are worded for someone who does not invest yet as much as for someone who does.
- **Then.** A follow-up, at the levels it names, asked in a message of its own once the *Ask* has
  a typed answer — never after *not sure yet*, *skip* or *explain*'s third step. Its typed words
  join the *Ask*'s as a further line of the same answer, under the same heading and the same ID;
  *skip* or *not sure yet* there adds nothing, and the answer stands as typed. *Teaches* follows
  the *Then*, or the *Ask* when no *Then* is asked.
- **Plain.** Said on *explain*: one plain line, then the question again in its plainest form — its
  Starter wording, or, for a question Starter does not ask, the question in *Plain*.
- **Example.** Said on *example*: two or three answers other people give, labelled as examples.
  - An example is never written.
  - An owner who says one again in their own words has typed it. A number, or *that one*, is a
    pick, not typed words.
- **Teaches.** One idea, said after any answer, *not sure yet* and *skip* included.
  - Starter names no paper.
  - Building names at most one work, as a lead.
  - Researching gives the placement and the other side.
- **Lands under.** The heading of `Philosophy/HOW-I-INVEST.md` where a typed answer goes — of
  `Philosophy/HOW-I-WORK.md` for a W card, section 10.
- **Behind it.** Shown at Researching only, or when the owner asks *show me the paper*.
  - These are the map's leads.
  - *A gap* means the map holds no work, and the gap is said out loud.
- **Presses on.** At Researching and on every retake: quote the owner's own line in
  `HOW-I-INVEST.md` that this question tests, if one clearly does. Never guess one.

## How every question is asked

- **Open questions are asked in chat**, because only typed words reach the file.
  - One question per message, and one thing per question: a second part is the card's *Then*.
    At Building and Researching, the owner may ask for a whole block at once.
  - Every open question opens with where the round stands, in one short line — *question 7 of 22
    · about 15 minutes left* — counting the IDs the level asks after O3, as section 3 gives them,
    a *Then* not counted, and the minutes left from the level's length; then its *Why*, then the
    *Ask*.
  - Every open question ends with the same line:
    > *Answer in your words — one line is enough. Or say: example · explain · not sure yet · skip.*
- **Pick questions are asked with the question tool** (`AskUserQuestion` in Claude Code): at most
  four options, plus *Other*.
  - Without a question tool (Codex), the question is one chat message, with the options
    numbered and *Other — your own words* last.
  - **A pick answered in words** — typed under *Other*, or in place of choosing — is read, never
    asked again. O3's words are mapped to the level they describe, said back, and the owner may
    change it. Under S1–S3 the words are the owner's own words for that question, written as the
    *in your own words?* line would be, and an option they also tick lands nowhere, as every pick.
- **After a hunch, O2, or the stance picks, S1–S4**, one optional line: *Want to say that in your
  own words? One line, or skip.* Only that typed line reaches the file. No other pick takes one:
  O3, O4, the calibration, the checkpoints and C2 land nowhere a typed line could go.
- **The escapes**, available on every question, are recognised in the owner's language and in
  their own wording too. A message that is only an escape is the escape and writes nothing; a
  message with more than that is a typed answer. When it could be either, ask once.

## 1 · Opening — every level, plain words for everyone

### O1 · Why you invest
- *Asked at:* every level, in chat.
- *Lands under:* **Why I invest**.
- **Why:** Your reason sets everything after it: how long you can wait, and what "doing well"
  means for you.
- **Ask:** Why do you invest, or why do you want to start? What would it change for you?
- **Plain:** This is about you, not about markets. Any reason counts.
- **Example:**
  - "So inflation doesn't eat my savings."
  - "To depend less on my salary one day."
  - "Markets fascinate me."
  - "It's my job."
- **Teaches:** Why you invest shapes almost everything after it: how long you can wait, how big a
  fall you can live with, and what *doing well* means for you.
- **Behind it:** none. This one is about you.

### O2 · What you already believe
- *Asked at:* every level, in chat.
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about markets**.
- **Why:** A belief written down can be checked later; one kept in your head only gets confirmed.
- **Ask:** What do you already believe about money and markets? Anything at all — a hunch, or
  something you heard and aren't sure of.
- **Plain:** Everyone has a few, and they don't have to be right. "It's a casino" counts.
- **If nothing comes** — *not sure yet*, or *example* — ask one multi-select tool question,
  `Hunches` (`Intuiciones`), once. The hunches are six of the seven beliefs of the map's *Beliefs
  people type*, in plain words, and one from its *Where a belief sits*, *No — prices already
  know*, said as the first below.
  - First call — three hunches and a fourth option, *More hunches*:
    - *Nobody can do better than the market as a whole*
    - *What goes up keeps going up*
    - *Bargain shares beat pricey ones*
  - On *More hunches*, a second call with the other four:
    - *Charts show where prices go*
    - *Prices overreact, and bubbles happen*
    - *Timing the big turns is what matters*
    - *Machines will find what people miss*
  - Without a question tool: one numbered list of all seven.
  - Picking none keeps *not sure yet*, a complete answer. A belief the owner types under *Other*,
    or later, is placed the same way — the belief that big players' money moves prices among them,
    the map's *Big players' flows move prices*, which the map places though the hunches leave it
    out.
  - Then ask: *Want to say that belief in your own words — and why, if you like? One line, or
    skip.* Only that line is written, never the hunch; it is the O2 line C1 quotes.
- **Teaches:** Almost every belief about markets has already been written down, tested and argued
  over by someone. Reading is how you find out by whom, and whether it held up.
- **Placement,** said after O3, once the level is known. It is never written to any file.
  - **Starter:** one plain line, no paper. *People have argued about this for a long time, and
    there's a well-known study on it; I'll suggest it at the end.*
  - **Building:** the work that holds the belief, and its other side, as *Beliefs people type* or
    *Where a belief sits* names them, as leads.
  - **Researching:** the act and the arc's question, the work that holds it, who tested it, and its
    other side as the map names it.
  - A belief neither entry lists is placed by the arc question whose works or core ideas name it,
    in *The six acts*. If the map names no other side, or places nothing, say so. Nothing is placed
    for *not sure yet*.

### O3 · How much you know
- *Asked at:* every level, as a single-select tool question.
- *Lands under:* nothing. The level goes only into the round file's first line.
- Say first: *This only sets how I word the next questions. Say "simpler" or "harder" at any
  time.*
- Header: `Experience` (`Experiencia`).
- **Ask:** How much do you know about investing today?
- **Options:**

| *Some experience* | I've bought funds, shares or crypto; index funds, diversification and fees are familiar. | Building |
| *I research* | I test ideas with data, read papers on markets, or work in finance. | Researching |
| *Not sure — help me place myself* | Pick the question you could answer today; nothing is checked. | the calibration below |

- **The suggested option.** One option is marked *(suggested)*, by the order the skill's *step 4*
  gives; it is never picked for the owner. On a retake, the last level is marked *(current)*.
- **Calibration, only after *Not sure*.**
  - Header: `Which fits?` (`¿Cuál va?`).
  - **Ask:** Which of these could you answer today, in a sentence? Pick one; nothing is checked.
  - **Options**, a short label and the question in its description:

| Label | Description shown | Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Doing nothing* | Where would my money be if I did nothing clever with it? | Starter |

  - Say the result, and let the owner change it.

### O4 · Your rules
- *Asked at:* round 1, every level, right after the explainer of section 2, as a multi-select tool
  question. On a retake, after O3 and any explainer said again, while *Non-negotiables* holds no
  rule, and otherwise only when the owner says *my rules*. Never in a home whose `AGENTS.md`
  predates it, as the skill's *step 1* says.
- *Lands under:* never `Philosophy/`, and no line in the round file. On the round's go, the rules
  it settles go under *Non-negotiables* in `RESEARCHER.md`, as the skill's *step 11* says; *skip*
  or *stop* leaves that section as it is.
- Say first: *One more thing before the questions. A process keeps a few rules, decided on a calm
  day; these are the ones I'll keep to with you.*
- Header: `Your rules` (`Tus reglas`).
- **Ask:** Which rules should I keep to with you? The question shows two, a plain line each, as
  they will be written, but for *my*, which the file gives as the researcher's name:
  - *Every result comes from a tested tool, never from my head.*
  - *An idea is written down before it is tested, and every claim names its source — one without
    a source is marked as a guess.*
- **Options:**

| Option | Description shown |
| --- | --- |
| *Keep these two* (recommended) | Both, as written above. |
| *Add one of my own* | A mistake you never want to repeat — one line, in chat, after. |
| *Change or drop one* | Which one, and how — one line, in chat, after. |
| *Add the two for strategies* | For when you test a strategy: *An idea gets real money only after a test written down beforehand, never after one good month.* *Every plan is challenged before it is tested, and every idea tried is counted, the failed ones too.* |

- **The two for strategies.** When *Here for* holds *Build and test a strategy*, they are shown
  with the two from the start, four lines, and *Keep these four* (recommended) takes the place
  of *Keep these two* and of *Add the two for strategies*. Otherwise *Add the two for
  strategies* is an option only when the owner asks for them, in any words.
- **What is kept.** Whatever is not changed or dropped is kept, so an add alone keeps the two, or
  the four. A rule typed under *Other* is a rule of the owner's own. No *in your own words?*
  follows O4: after *Keep these two* or *Keep these four* alone nothing more is asked, and after
  *Add one of my own* or *Change or drop one* only its one line in chat.
- **A rule about holdings** — how much in one place, when to sell, when to look again — is not
  written under *Non-negotiables*, where no brief reads it. Say in one line that it belongs in
  `Portfolio/RULES.md`, the file `brief` quotes, which `brief setup` starts and the owner writes;
  the preview shows it apart, and the hand-over names `brief setup`. `philosophy` never writes in
  `Portfolio/`.
- **A home with rules already** — from an older interview, or added with *remember this* — shows
  them first, word for word, and they stay unless *Change or drop one* names one. When they hold
  every rule shown in any words — an older template's three count — *Keep my rules (current)*
  takes the place of *Keep these two* or *Keep these four*. When they hold some but not all,
  *Keep my rules (current)* is offered first, and *Keep mine, add the rest* names each rule it
  would add, word for word; when they hold the two for strategies, *Add the two for strategies*
  is left out. A rule held in any words is never added again.
- **Plain:** A rule is a choice made before the moment it is for, so the moment can't make it for
  you.
- **Plain words only.** Every rule is written as plainly as here, in the owner's language — never a
  strategy's term the owner did not use: engines, a book, an analyzer measurement.

## 2 · The idea the rest builds on (said once, after O3, never asked)

Each version is neutral: it settles nothing the owner answers later, *can anyone do better than
the market?* least of all. The purpose line is the same in all three, and the only one.

### Starter

> One idea first, because everything after builds on it.
>
> Everyone who saves already has a yardstick, even without a name for it: what the money would do
> if you did nothing clever with it. It might sit in the bank, in a pension, or in a fund that buys
> a little of every big company, so you don't have to choose. I'll call it your **personal
> benchmark**.
>
> Every investment choice after that is a bet that you can do better than it, after fees. A
> well-known study of professional funds suggests it's hard; whether it can be done is one of the
> things you'll decide for yourself.
>
> Either way, that's what a **process** is for:
> - write down what you believe, and why;
> - say what you'll compare it with;
> - test it before money is at stake;
> - decide what you'll do before you feel it.
>
> I'm here so you can make better investment decisions, by your own yardstick.
>
> One honest caveat: none of the studies I start from shows that a process makes more money. A
> process is how you find out whether a result is yours, or luck.

If the owner asks which study: the map's Jensen, 1968, *The Performance of Mutual Funds in the
Period 1945–1964*, which it sums up as most active managers failing to generate persistent alpha.
That is a lead to read, not something cited yet. *The studies I start from* are the reading map's.

### Building

> Everything below is measured against one thing: your **personal benchmark**, what your money
> would do with no decisions, tied to your goal. Whether anyone can do better than it, after costs,
> is one of the questions you'll answer — the reading map holds both sides.
>
> A **process** is how you find out whether you did: what you believe written down, a yardstick
> chosen in advance, ideas tested before money is at stake.
>
> I'm here so you can make better investment decisions, by your own yardstick. Nothing on the
> reading map shows that a process makes more money; it's how you find out whether a result is
> yours, or luck.

### Researching

> These questions follow the papers of the map's two timelines, each named by what it is about:
> - the claim (Fama, 1970);
> - the counterparty (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979);
> - the cost of knowing (Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980);
> - the limits of arbitrage (Shleifer & Vishny, 1997);
> - adaptation (Lo, 2004);
> - then the evidence from 2011 to 2023: crowding, cycles, decay, false positives, and the
>   rebuttal.
>
> Your **personal benchmark** is your own yardstick, tied to your goal; a strategy's benchmark is
> chosen later, at part E, and is a different thing. Whether anyone does better than yours after
> costs is a question you'll answer, not one I assume.
>
> I'm here so you can make better investment decisions, by your own yardstick. The map holds no
> paper showing that a process makes more money. A process is how you find out whether a result is
> yours, or luck.

**Checkpoints.** After each block except the last, ask one tool question, `Continue?`
(`¿Seguimos?`):
- *Next block (N questions)*
- *Save and stop here*
- *Simpler from here* — not at Starter

- *About right — next block*

**Moving between levels.**
- Moving up or down switches to that level's set for the remaining blocks.
- Nothing already answered is asked again.
- The round file records the level the round ended at.

**A single question can rise on its own.** If the work *Behind it* now has a note in the owner's
library, ask that question one level higher and link the note, even inside a lower level. Rising
changes the wording of a question the level asks; it never adds one the level does not ask.

## 4 · The questions

### Your scoreboard

#### Q2 · Your goal
- *Asked at:* every level; at Starter, only when O1 named no goal or no date. A Starter round that
  does not ask it gives it no line, and no retake counts it as never reached.
- *Lands under:* **Why I invest**.
- **Why:** Money needed in two years and money needed in twenty can't be judged the same way.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** a one-line follow-up to O1, first in the block: *You said "<O1>". And by when?* —
    *What for, and by when?* when O1 named no goal, without the quote when it has no typed words.
  - **Building and Researching:** What is this money for, and by when? If it is for several
    things, name each, with its date.
- **Then:**
  - **Building:** What happens if it isn't there in time?
  - **Researching:** Which yardstick follows from that goal?
- **Plain:** A goal with a date, big or small.
- **Example:**
  - "A deposit for a flat in five years."
  - "An income from age 60."
  - "Nothing specific yet; I want it to grow."
- **Teaches:** The date matters as much as the goal. Money you need in two years has less time to
  wait out a bad year than money for twenty.
- **Behind it:** *a gap*. The map holds no work on goals or goal-based benchmarks. Say so, and
  propose nothing.

#### Q1 · Your personal benchmark
- *Asked at:* every level.
- *Lands under:* **Why I invest**.
- **Why:** You can only say you did well against something; this is that something, for your own
  money.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** Suppose you never chose an investment on purpose. Where would your savings sit — a
    bank account, a pension, cash at home? If you have none yet, where would you put the first?
  - **Building:** What do you compare your results against — a savings rate, a market index, a
    mix? Why that one?
  - **Researching:** If you did nothing clever with this money, where would it be? That is your
    personal benchmark: a place, not a target to beat.
- **Then:**
  - **Researching:** What risk does it carry, and why is it the right comparison for your goal?
- **Plain:** Imagine you never pick an investment on purpose. Where does the money sit: a bank
  account, a pension, lending to the government (bonds), cash, a fund that buys a little of every
  big company?
- **Example:**
  - "In my savings account."
  - "In my company pension fund."
  - "In a fund that buys a little of every big company."
- **Teaches:**
  - **Every level:** That is your personal benchmark. Every choice from here is a bet you can do
    better than it. You choose it, and you can change it.
  - **After *not sure yet* or *skip*, instead:** Wherever your money sits when you do nothing
    clever with it is your personal benchmark. Every choice from here is a bet you can do better
    than it.
  - **Building adds:** A personal benchmark isn't a strategy's benchmark; a strategy picks its own

#### Q3 · How you would know

#### Q4 · The other side
- *Asked at:* Building and Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about markets**.
- **Why:** Every gain on a trade is someone else's loss on it, so an idea needs a reason the other
  side keeps losing.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Building and Researching:** When you buy, someone sells to you. Why would you be right and
    they be wrong?
- **Then:**
  - **Researching:** Who is that seller, usually — and what keeps them selling to you?
- **Plain:** Every trade has a buyer and a seller, and both think they're right.
- **Example:**
  - "I can wait ten years; many sellers need their money next month."
  - "I don't know — that's what I want to find out."
- **Teaches:** An idea with no answer to *who is on the other side?* is only a pattern. The map's
  *Five sources of edge* names five places an edge can come from — hints to check, not rules:
  - being paid for a risk others avoid;
  - others repeating a mistake;
  - others who can't trade against it;
  - seeing something the price hasn't taken in;
  - doing it better, after costs.
- **Behind it:**
  - Fama, 1970, and Kahneman & Tversky, 1979 (Start here 1–2).
  - Shleifer & Vishny, 1997 (Start here 4).
  - *Five sources of edge*.
  - *Seven questions before any backtest*, question 2.

#### Q5 · What it costs to know
- *Asked at:* Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about markets**.
- **Why:** An edge that costs more to find than it earns is a loss, however real it is.
- **Ask:** What does it cost you to know what you know — in time, data and tools?
- **Then:** Is your edge worth more than that, after costs?
- **Plain:** What do you spend (hours, subscriptions, software) to find your ideas, and do they pay
  you back more than that?
- **Example:** "Two evenings a week and a data subscription; I don't know yet whether it pays."
- **Teaches:** The map sums up Grossman & Stiglitz (1980) like this: if information costs money,
  prices cannot be perfect; somebody has to be paid to make them right. Its practical version,
  Pedersen (2015): just enough inefficiency to pay the active managers who compete it away. Both
  are leads to read, not settled facts.
- **Behind it:**
  - Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980 (Start here 3). The question is Act III, question 09's edge line,
    put to the owner.
  - Pedersen, 2015, *Efficiently Inefficient* (*Nothing is discarded*).
  - **Other side:** Fama, 1970; Berk & Green, 2004.

### You

#### Q6 · When it loses
- *Asked at:* every level.
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Why:** What you do after a loss is decided either on a calm day or in the moment; this asks
  which.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** Imagine you put 1,000 into something and a month later it's worth 800. What would
    you do? Would you decide that now, or only when it happens?
  - **Building and Researching:** When an idea loses money, what do you do?
- **Then:**
  - **Building and Researching:** Did you decide that before it happened, or when it happened?
- **Plain:** Most people have never decided this in advance. That's the point of asking.
- **Example:**
  - "Sell, so it stops hurting."
  - "Wait; I planned to hold for years."
  - "Buy more, if I still believe in it."
- **Teaches:** Here's an idea from a well-known study, worth testing on yourself: losing tends to
  hurt more than winning feels good, so a choice made in the moment is often not the one you'd
  make on a calm day. Deciding beforehand is the protection.
  - **Building adds:** the lead is Kahneman & Tversky (1979).
- **Behind it:** Kahneman & Tversky, 1979, *Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk*
  (Start here 2).

#### Q7 · How long you can hold
- *Asked at:* Building and Researching.
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Why:** Being right too late costs as much as being wrong; this asks how long you can wait.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Building and Researching:** How long could you hold an idea you still believe in while it
    loses — weeks, months, years?
- **Then:**
  - **Building:** What would make you stop?
  - **Researching:** What would make you stop — and which runs out first: money, patience, or
    time?
- **Plain** (the plainest form): If you still believed in an investment but it kept losing, how
  long could you wait? What would make you give up?
- **Example:**
  - "A year; after that I'd need the money."
  - "As long as the reason I bought is still true."
- **Teaches:** Being right too late can cost as much as being wrong. Money, patience or time can
  run out before an idea pays.
  - **Building adds:** The map sums up Shleifer & Vishny (1997) like this: seeing the mispricing
    is not holding the trade; capital, risk and horizon run out first. A lead to read.
- **Behind it:**
  - Shleifer & Vishny, 1997, *The Limits of Arbitrage* (Start here 4).
  - *Seven questions before any backtest*, question 6: "When does it break, and would I stay in
    through the break?"

#### Q8 · Fear and excitement
- *Asked at:* every level.
- *Lands under:* **What I have learned**.
- **Why:** Knowing your own reflex is what lets a plan, rather than the moment, decide.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** When you hear about a market crash on the news, or a friend brags about a big
    win, what do you feel like doing? If it has never happened to you, what do you think you'd
    do?
  - **Building and Researching:** When you feel fear or excitement about a market, what do you do
    with it?
- **Then:**
  - **Researching:** In which market did that reflex serve you, and in which did it cost you?
- **Plain:** Think of a time a headline made you want to act right away.
- **Example:**
  - "I check prices all day and sometimes sell."
  - "I buy what friends buy."
  - "I switch off the news."
- **Teaches:** No habit here is wrong; knowing yours is the point. A reflex that served you in one
  setting can mislead you in another, and a plan written on a calm day lets you act on the plan
  instead.
  - **Building adds:** The map's *Nothing is discarded* gives that idea to behavioral finance —
    Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler: shortcuts that worked in another environment. A lead to read.
- **Behind it:**
  - Kahneman & Tversky, 1979 (Start here 2).
  - *Nothing is discarded*, its behavioral finance entry — Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler: shortcuts
    that worked in another environment. It names no work of Thaler's, so none is proposed.

### The world moves

#### Q9 · A very different market
- *Asked at:* Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about markets**.
- **Why:** An idea that worked in one kind of market may only be a story about that market.
- **Ask:** Take the idea you rely on most. Would it have worked in a crash? In a boom? With rates
  rising fast? A guess for each is enough.
- **Then:** In which of them does it break, and how would you see that coming?
- **Plain:** Picture the worst year you can remember in markets. Would your idea have survived it?
- **Example:** "Probably not in a crash; it needs rising prices."
- **Teaches:** The map sums up Lo (2004) like this: edges get crowded and decay, the environment
  shifts, new ones appear. A lead to read, not a settled fact. An idea that worked in only one
  kind of market may be a story about that market.
- **Behind it:**
  - Lo, 2004, *The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis* (Start here 5).
  - Daniel & Moskowitz, 2016, *Momentum Crashes* (Start here 7).
  - Act VII, problem 1: regimes you can see coming. It names Hamilton (1989) and Ang &
    Timmermann (2012) without titles, so neither is proposed.

#### Q10 · Once everybody knows
- *Asked at:* Building and Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about markets**.
- **Why:** Ideas that get published or crowded can stop paying; this asks whether yours could.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Building and Researching:** What happens to an idea once everybody knows it, or everybody
    holds it? Think of one of yours.
- **Then:**
  - **Researching:** How much of it would be left, and how long would it last?
- **Plain:** If a trick for beating the market were in every newspaper, would it still work?
- **Example:**
  - "It stops working, because everybody's already in."
  - "It keeps working if the reason behind it doesn't change."
- **Teaches:**
  - **The plain form:** An idea many people know may get priced in, and a crowded one can fall
    hard when everyone sells at once — two ideas to check, not facts.
  - **Building and Researching:** The map sums up McLean & Pontiff (2016) like this: anomaly
    returns roughly halve after publication. A lead to read, not a settled fact.
  - **Researching adds:** And Khandani & Lo (2011), as the map sums it up: quant books that shared
    a strategy unwound together in three days. A lead to read too.
- **Behind it:**
  - McLean & Pontiff, 2016, *Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?* (Start
    here 8).
  - Khandani & Lo, 2011, *What Happened to the Quants in August 2007? Evidence from Factors and
    Transactions Data* (Start here 6).
  - *Discussion*, its second question: how long a strategy would last if alpha decays.

#### Q11 · The turn, or the conditions
- *Asked at:* Researching.
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Why:** Some losses come before the turn, not after it; this asks what you watch.
- **Ask:** When a trend changes, do you act on the turn itself — or earlier, on the signs that a
  turn would hurt, such as a big fall or a jump in volatility?
- **Plain:** Do you move when a trend turns, or do you watch for the signs that a turn would hurt?
- **Example:**
  - "I move when it turns."
  - "I cut risk after a big fall, before any turn."
- **Teaches:** The map sums up Daniel & Moskowitz (2016) like this: momentum crashes in sharp
  rebounds — a strategy works, gets crowded, breaks, and comes back. A lead to read, not a settled
  fact. If it holds, the state before a turn may matter as much as the turn.
- **Behind it:**
  - Daniel & Moskowitz, 2016 (Start here 7).
  - **Other side, on timing:** Grinold & Kahn, 1995, *Active Portfolio Management*: many small
    bets beat a few big calls, as *Beliefs people type* gives it.

### Your proof

#### Q12 · How many you tried
- *Asked at:* Building and Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What would change my mind**.
- **Why:** Try enough ideas and one looks good by luck alone; the count is how you know the bar.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Building and Researching:** How many ideas did you try before the one you keep — or, if you
    haven't started, how will you count them?
- **Then:**
  - **Researching:** Had you tried twice as many, would you trust the one you kept any less?
- **Plain:** Toss enough coins and one will land heads ten times in a row.
- **Example:**
  - "Just one."
  - "Dozens; I never counted."
- **Teaches:** The more ideas you try, the likelier one looks good by luck alone, so the bar rises
  for the one you keep.
  - **Building adds:** the map's lead on this is Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016).
- **Behind it:**
  - Harvey, Liu & Zhu, 2016, *… and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns* (Start here 9; Act V,
    question 17).
  - *Seven questions before any backtest*, question 7: "How many ideas came before this one?"

#### Q13 · Luck or skill
- *Asked at:* Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What would change my mind**.
- **Why:** A result you could never call luck can't be tested, and what can't be tested can't be
  improved.
- **Ask:** Suppose a strategy of yours did well last year. What would you need to see to conclude
  it was luck, not skill?
- **Plain:** If a friend made money last year, how would you tell whether it was skill?
- **Example:**
  - "If it stopped working the year after I started using it."
  - "Nothing — and that worries me."
- **Teaches:** If nothing could convince you, the result can't be tested, and what can't be tested
  can't be improved.
- **Behind it:** Harvey, Liu & Zhu, 2016, against Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, 2023, *Is There a
  Replication Crisis in Finance?* Still contested (*Who argues with whom*).

#### Q14 · Written down
- *Asked at:* every level.
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Why:** A rule only you can run isn't a rule yet; writing it down is the cheapest test.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** If you invest, or when you start, would you write down your rules — when to buy,
    how much, when to sell — clearly enough that a friend could follow them and do exactly the
    same?
  - **Building:** Is your rule written down so that someone else would get the same answer?
  - **Researching:** Is it written down before the test, so that someone else would get the same
    answer?
- **Plain:** A recipe someone else can cook is a rule. "A pinch of this" is not.
- **Example:**
  - "No, it's in my head."
  - "I buy the same fund every month, whatever happens."
- **Teaches:** A rule that gives two answers when you run it twice isn't a rule yet. Writing it
  down is the first step of a process, and the cheapest.
- **Behind it:**
  - **Starter and Building:** no paper.
  - **Researching:** Arnott, Harvey & Markowitz, 2019, *A Backtesting Protocol in the Era of
    Machine Learning* (under Act V, question 17).
  - **Researching:** *Seven questions before any backtest*, its test: "if the seven answers do not
    fit in one paragraph, the idea is not yet a hypothesis".

are never asked below Researching.

- *What my reading touched* — suggested when a note read since is a work section 8 lists and no
  question is unreached, and left out when none is. It takes the IDs section 8 gives for the notes
  read since, in block order, then C1 and C2.
- *The whole round* — every question of the level chosen.
- *Only what I left open* — suggested when a question is unreached, and left out when none is
  open. It takes the questions whose latest status is *not sure yet*, *skipped* or *explained*, and
  those the level chosen asks that no round file has a line for, in block order, then C2.
- *Just show me what changed* — no questions, and nothing written.

*Not now* or *stop*, typed under *Other*, ends the run with nothing written.

### The level
O3 is asked again, except for *Just show me what changed*. The last level is marked *(current)*;
the level above it is marked *(suggested)* when two works from the last round's reading list now
have notes, never more than one level up. The explainer of section 2 is said again only when the
level changed, or when the owner asks.

### Each question asked again
- **Answered before:** quote the earlier answer, word for word, and its round, then ask:
  > *Still true? Type* keep*, or say it again in new words.*
- **Pick questions** are asked afresh: a pick is never recorded. The owner's own words for one, if
  a round has them, are quoted as for any answer.
- **Left open** (not sure yet, skipped or explained): asked first in its block, marked *(open since
  round N)*.
- **Never reached** — no line in any round file, as a round stopped early leaves it: asked in its
  place in the block, as in round 1. Typed words for it take the label *new*.
- **New at this level:** marked *(new at this level)*.

### The comparison
It is shown in chat before the preview, and never written to a file.

- **One line of counts:** answers kept, changed, new and still open; the level then and now; the
  notes read in between.
- **A table:** ID · the question in short · then (round, date) · now · kept / changed / new / still
  open. A question answered before and skipped now shows *not asked again*: its earlier answer
  stands.
- **Under each changed answer:** the notes read between the two rounds whose work is that
  question's *Behind it*, with this line:
  > *You read X between the two rounds; whether it moved you is yours to say.*
- **Conflicts:** any standing line in `HOW-I-INVEST.md` that today's answer cuts against is quoted,
  with *edit it by hand, or run `refine`, if it no longer holds*. The preview offers *Leave my old
  line — I'll fix it with `refine`* for each changed answer.

## 10 · Another field

For a round about how the owner works — law, medicine, teaching, a trade, a business — or another
topic they name, asked as the skill's *step 2* says and run as its *Another field* says. Everything
above holds but what is about markets, with `HOW-I-WORK.md` where it says `HOW-I-INVEST.md`: only
typed words are written, no answer is right or wrong, words for the owner never mark their gender,
and the escapes work. Section 9 holds as if the reading map were missing, so no work from the map
is named, and C2 is this section's own, below. There is no level, no explainer, no hunch, no stance
pick, no placement and no *Behind it*: each card is worded once, in plain words. In a field round,
*simpler* puts the *Plain* line and the *Example* under every question left, and *harder* is
answered with the rest of the round in one message.

### Before the first question (said, not asked)

> No test, and no right answers. I'll ask why you do this work, what you believe about it and the
> rules you want kept, then a few questions on how you work — about ten minutes, in one go. Say
> *skip* or *stop* any time; *not sure yet* is a complete answer.
>
> Only what you type goes into `Philosophy/HOW-I-WORK.md`, word for word, and the rules you keep
> into `RESEARCHER.md`, after you've seen them and said go. Take this again after you've read
> something, and you'll see how your view moved.

Then `Ready?`, as after section 0. On a retake, use the opening in section 7, as *A retake* below
says.

### W1 · Why you do it
- *Lands under:* **Why I do this work**.
- **Why:** Your reason decides what doing it well means to you.
- **Ask:** Why do you do this work, or why do you want to? Who is it for, and what would doing it
  well change?
- **Plain:** This is about you, not about your field. Any reason counts.
- **Example:** "So people get a fair hearing." · "I like the problems nobody else wants." · "It
  pays the bills, and I want it to do more than that."
- **Teaches:** Why you do it decides what *doing it well* means to you: the yardstick the rest of
  your answers lean on.

### W2 · What you believe about your field
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about my field**.
- **Why:** A belief written down can be checked; one kept in your head only gets confirmed.
- **Ask:** What do you believe about your field that not everyone in it would agree with?
- **Plain:** A hunch counts, and it doesn't have to be right.
- **Example:** "Most disputes are settled before anyone reaches a courtroom." · "Experience
  beats credentials."
- **Teaches:** A belief written down can be checked against what you read and what happens; one
  kept in your head only gets confirmed.

**O4 follows W2**, its card as written — on a retake, after W2 while *Non-negotiables* holds no
rule, otherwise on *my rules*. It is said first as: *One more thing before the questions: a few
rules, decided on a calm day — the ones I'll keep to with you.* The two for strategies are offered
only when the owner asks for them.

### W3 · What you have learned
- *Lands under:* **What I have learned**.
- **Why:** A lesson written down can't be quietly rewritten by memory.
- **Ask:** What has experience taught you that you would tell yourself ten years ago?
- **Plain:** One lesson is enough; a mistake you won't repeat counts.
- **Example:** "Read the whole file before forming a view." · "Ask what people need, not only what
  they asked for."
- **Teaches:** A lesson is evidence you already own; written down, memory can't quietly rewrite it.

### W4 · How you decide
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Why:** A way of deciding you can name is one you can improve.
- **Ask:** When the evidence is mixed, how do you decide — what do you need to see, whom do you
  ask, and when do you stop looking?
- **Plain:** Think of the last hard call you made at work.
- **Example:** "I write the best argument against my position first." · "I sleep on anything I
  can't explain in two sentences."
- **Teaches:** A way of deciding you can name is one you can improve; one you can't name repeats
  its mistakes quietly.

### W5 · What would change your mind
- *Lands under:* **What would change my mind**.
- **Why:** A belief you would drop on evidence is one you can test.
- **Ask:** You said you believe "<the line typed for W2, quoted>". What would you need to see to
  stop believing it? With no W2 line, ask about any belief on their work they'd like to test, or
  skip.
- **Plain:** A belief you could drop on evidence is one you can test.
- **Example:** "If the cases I settle early turned out worse for the people I represent." · "If
  newcomers with other training kept doing better than me."
- **Teaches:** A belief you'd drop on evidence is one you can test. Your answer here is the line
  I'll quote first when you take this again.
