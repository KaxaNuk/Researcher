## How a card reads

- **ID.** It stays the same across levels and rounds.
- **Asked at.** Starter (beginner), Building (intermediate) or Researching (advanced). The levels
  are nested: each asks everything the one below asks, plus more.
- **Short.** The question in at most eight words, in the owner's language, written in bold in
  front of the owner's answer in `HOW-I-INVEST.md` so the line still makes sense a year later. A
  label, never the owner's view.
- **Why.** One plain sentence said before the *Ask*. Never written.
- **Ask.** The question: one thing to answer, worded as a conversation, by level where it says so.
- **Then.** A follow-up at the levels it names, asked in a message of its own once the *Ask* has a
  typed answer. Its typed words join the *Ask*'s as a further line of the same answer.
- **Plain.** Said on *explain*: one plain line, then the question again in its plainest form.
- **Example.** Said on *example*: two or three answers other people give, never written.
- **Teaches.** One idea, said after any answer, *not sure yet* and *skip* included. Starter names
  no work; Building names at most one, as a lead; Researching gives the lead and its other side.
  Never a verdict on the answer.
- **Lands under.** The heading of `HOW-I-INVEST.md` where a typed answer goes.
- **Behind it.** The map's leads, shown at Researching or when the owner asks *show me the paper*.
  *A gap* means the map holds no work, and the gap is said out loud.

## Your story — every level, plain words

### O1 · Why you invest
- *Lands under:* **Why I invest**.
- **Short:** *Why I invest* (*Por qué invierto*).
- **Why:** Your reason sets everything after it: how long you can wait, and what doing well means.
- **Ask:** Why do you invest — or why do you want to start? What do you want that money to do for
  you?
- **Plain:** This is about you, not about markets. Any reason counts.
- **Example:**
  - "So inflation doesn't eat my savings."
  - "To depend less on my salary one day."
  - "It's my job, and markets fascinate me."
- **Teaches:** Why you invest shapes almost everything after it: how long you can wait, how big a
  fall you can live with, and what *doing well* means for you.
- **Behind it:** none. This one is about you.

### Q18 · For whom, and by when
- *Lands under:* **Why I invest**.
- **Short:** *Whose money, and by when* (*De quién es el dinero y para cuándo*).
- **Why:** Money needed in two years, or someone else's, can't be run like your own savings for
  twenty.
- **Ask:** Tell me whose money it is — yours, your family's, other people's — and when each part
  will be needed.
- **Then** (Building and Researching): How do you run it when it's someone else's, or needed
  sooner?
- **Plain:** Your savings, your children's school, a fund: each has its own date.
- **Example:**
  - "Mine, for retirement in twenty-five years."
  - "Mine and my parents'; theirs I keep much safer."
- **Teaches:** The date matters as much as the goal, and whose money it is changes how big a fall
  it can take. Rules written once, per pool, keep one pool's urgency from deciding for another.
- **Behind it:** Berk & Green, 2004, *Mutual Fund Flows and Performance in Rational Markets*:
  "alpha exists" is not "the client gets it" (Researching).
- **Never:** a holding, an account, a broker or a person's name is asked for; a typed one is
  marked in the preview, as the skill's *Privacy* line says.

### Q15 · Your best and worst decision
- *Lands under:* **What I have learned**.
- **Short:** *My best and worst money decision* (*Mi mejor y mi peor decisión con dinero*).
- **Why:** A decision you can tell as a story is the best evidence of how you really decide.
- **Ask:** Tell me about your best and your worst decision with money. What happened, and what
  did you learn?
- **Plain:** Any money decision counts — an investment, a purchase, a loan, or one you didn't
  make.
- **Example:**
  - "I bought what a friend recommended and sold in a panic. Now I decide when to sell before I
    buy."
  - "Best: I kept saving every month through the crash, without looking."
- **Teaches:** Making the next decision better than the last, on purpose, is what a process is
  for. A lesson written down as a story is one memory can't quietly rewrite.
- **Behind it:** *a gap*; no paper.

## The idea the rest builds on (said once, after O3, never asked)

### Starter

> One idea first, because the next questions build on it.
>
> Everyone who saves already has a yardstick, even without a name for it: what the money would do
> if you did nothing clever with it — in the bank, in a pension, in a fund that buys a little of
> every big company. I'll call it your **personal benchmark**. Every investment choice after that
> is a bet you can do better than it, after fees.
>
> A **process** is how you find out whether you did: write down what you believe, say what you'll
> compare it with, test it before money is at stake, decide what you'll do before you feel it.
>
> I'm here so you can make better investment decisions, by your own yardstick. One honest caveat:
> a process doesn't promise more money; it's how you tell a result that's yours from luck.

> Everything below is measured against your **personal benchmark**: what your money would do with

## Your yardstick — every level

### Q1 · What you measure against
- *Lands under:* **Why I invest**.
- **Short:** *What I measure against* (*Contra qué me mido*).
- **Why:** You can only say you did well against something; this is that something, for your own
  money.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** If you never chose an investment on purpose, where would your money sit — a bank
    account, a pension, a government bond? That's what you'd compare against.
  - **Building:** What do you compare your results against, and in which currency?
  - **Researching:** If you did nothing clever with this money, where would it be — and in which
    currency do you count?
- **Then** (Building and Researching): Over how many years, and after which costs, would you say
  you beat it?
- **Plain:** Imagine you never pick an investment. Where does the money sit?
- **Example:**
  - "In my savings account."
  - "Government bonds in pesos, plus ten points."
  - "A fund that buys a little of every big company, in dollars."
- **Teaches:** That is your personal benchmark: every choice from here is a bet you can do better
  than it, after costs. The currency you'll spend in is part of it, and one good year proves
  little — luck can do that.
  - **Building adds:** Jensen (1968) measured fund managers against such a yardstick — a lead to
    read.
  - **Researching adds:** and Cowles (1933) put forecasters to the test; Berk & Green (2004) is
    the other side.
- **Behind it:** Cowles, 1933, *Can Stock Market Forecasters Forecast?*; Jensen, 1968, *The
  Performance of Mutual Funds in the Period 1945–1964*; Fama, 1970, *Efficient Capital Markets: A
  Review of Theory and Empirical Work*; Sharpe, 1964, *Capital Asset Prices*. **Other side:** Berk
  & Green, 2004. The map holds no work on a benchmark's currency: *a gap*.
- **Never:** a product, a ticker, an allocation or a computed return.

### Q6 · When it falls
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Short:** *What I do when it falls 20%* (*Qué hago si cae 20%*).
- **Why:** What you do after a fall is decided either on a calm day or in the moment; this asks
  which.
- **Ask:** Imagine what you hold falls 20% in a month. What do you do?
- **Then:** When would you decide that — on a calm day before it happens, or in the moment?
- **Plain:** 1,000 becomes 800 in a month. Sell, wait, buy more, or look away?
- **Example:**
  - "Sell, so it stops hurting."
  - "Wait; I planned to hold for years."
  - "Buy more, if the reason I bought still holds."
- **Teaches:** Losing tends to hurt more than winning feels good, so a choice made in the moment is
  often not the one you'd make on a calm day. Deciding beforehand is the protection.
  - **Building adds:** the lead is Kahneman & Tversky (1979).
  - **Researching adds:** and Shleifer & Vishny (1997): money, patience or time can run out before
    an idea pays.
- **Behind it:** Kahneman & Tversky, 1979, *Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk*;
  Shleifer & Vishny, 1997, *The Limits of Arbitrage*.

## Your edge — Building and Researching

### Q4 · Where your edge is
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about markets**.
- **Short:** *Where my edge is* (*Dónde está mi ventaja*).
- **Why:** Every trade has someone on the other side; an idea needs a reason you'd be right and
  they wrong.
- **Ask:** When you buy, someone sells to you. Why would you be right — where does your edge come
  from?
- **Then** (Researching): How long does it last once others see it too?
- **Plain:** What do you know, see or do that the person selling to you doesn't?
- **Example:**
  - "I can wait ten years; many sellers need their money next month."
  - "Big flows push prices away from value, and I can study them."
  - "I don't know — that's what I want to find out."
- **Teaches:** An idea with no answer to *who is on the other side?* is only a pattern. The map
  names five places an edge can come from — hints to check, not rules: a risk others avoid, a
  mistake others repeat, others who can't trade against it, something the price hasn't taken in,
  doing it better after costs.
  - **Building adds:** one lead, the work that holds the source the owner named, as the map's
    *Where a belief sits* gives it.
  - **Researching adds:** its other side, and Lo (2004) and McLean & Pontiff (2016): edges get
    crowded and decay.
- **Behind it:** the map's *Five sources of edge*; Fama, 1970; Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Grossman
  & Stiglitz, 1980, *On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets*; Shleifer &
  Vishny, 1997; Ross, 1976, *The Arbitrage Theory of Capital Asset Pricing*; Lo, 2004, *The
  Adaptive Markets Hypothesis*; McLean & Pontiff, 2016, *Does Academic Research Destroy Stock
  Return Predictability?*; Pedersen, 2015, *Efficiently Inefficient*.

### Q17 · How you split the money
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Short:** *How I split the money* (*Cómo reparto el dinero*).
- **Why:** Which ideas you hold is half the story; how much goes to each, and when it changes, is
  the other half.
- **Ask:** When you have several ideas or asset classes, how do you decide how much goes to each?
- **Then:** What tells you to change that split — a date, a result, a change in the market?
- **Plain:** Three good ideas and one pot of money: how do you split it?
- **Example:**
  - "Equal parts, rebalanced once a year."
  - "More to what tends to fail at different times from the rest."
- **Teaches:** How you combine bets can matter as much as which bets you pick: two ideas that fail
  at different times can be steadier together than either alone.
  - **Building adds:** Markowitz (1952) made that the starting point of portfolio choice — a lead.
  - **Researching adds:** and Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013): combine premia that fail at
    different times.
- **Behind it:** Markowitz, 1952, *Portfolio Selection*; Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, 2013, *Value
  and Momentum Everywhere*; Grinold & Kahn, 1995, *Active Portfolio Management*.
- **Never:** an allocation is proposed; the question asks how the owner decides, not what to hold.

## Your proof — Researching

### Q12 · How you'd prove it's skill
- *Lands under:* **What would change my mind**.
- **Short:** *How I'd prove it's skill, not luck* (*Cómo probaría que es habilidad*).
- **Why:** Try enough ideas and one looks good by luck alone; a proof names its bar in advance.
- **Ask:** Suppose your process does well for a year. What would you need to see — over how long,
  against what — to call it skill, not luck?
- **Then:** How many ideas did you try before the ones you keep, and do you count them?
- **Plain:** Toss enough coins and one lands heads ten times in a row.
- **Example:**
  - "Five years ahead of my benchmark after costs, written down before I start."
  - "I never counted the ideas I dropped — I should."
- **Teaches:** The more ideas you try, the likelier one looks good by luck, so the bar rises for
  the one you keep. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) argue most published findings are false; Jensen,
  Kelly & Pedersen (2023) that most hold up when retested — still contested, both leads.
- **Behind it:** Harvey, Liu & Zhu, 2016, *… and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns*; Jensen,
  Kelly & Pedersen, 2023, *Is There a Replication Crisis in Finance?*; Arnott, Harvey & Markowitz,
  2019, *A Backtesting Protocol in the Era of Machine Learning*.

### Q16 · How you keep it running
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Short:** *How I keep my process running* (*Cómo mantengo mi proceso*).
- **Why:** A process you can't keep every week is a plan; this asks what keeping it takes.
- **Ask:** What does your process need every week to keep running — time, data, tools, people —
  and what do you watch to know it still works?
- **Plain:** What would make it easy to stick to a plan every week?
- **Example:**
  - "An hour on Sundays, one place where all my data lives."
  - "A daily check of each strategy against its benchmark."
- **Teaches:** A process you can't keep is a plan, not a process. What you watch decides what you
  can catch: a strategy can work, get crowded, break and come back.
- **Behind it:** Daniel & Moskowitz, 2016, *Momentum Crashes*; Guo, Wang, Ni & Shum, 2022, *Quant
  4.0: Engineering Quantitative Investment with Automated, Explainable and Knowledge-driven AI*.

## Closing — every level

### C1 · What would change your mind
- *Lands under:* **What would change my mind**.
- **Short:** *What would change how I invest* (*Qué me haría cambiar cómo invierto*).
- **Why:** A view you would drop on evidence is one you can test.
- **Ask:** You said "<the owner's Q4 line, or else their O1 line, quoted>". What would you need to
  see to change how you invest?
- **Plain:** If you were wrong about it, how would you find out?
- **Example:**
  - "If my strategies trailed my benchmark for three years after costs."
  - "If I learned most professionals can't do it either."
- **Teaches:** Naming what would end a view turns it from an opinion into something you can test.
  This is the line I'll quote first when you take this again.
- **Behind it:** the map's *Discussion* and *An idea's anatomy*, the claim and the kill switch;
  `objective` takes the shape further, in a strategy.

open; the level then and now; the notes read in between), then a table — ID · *Short* · then
(round, date) · now · label. Under each changed answer, the notes read between the two rounds whose
work is that question's *Behind it*: *You read X between the two rounds; whether it moved you is
yours to say.* A standing line in `HOW-I-INVEST.md` that today's answer cuts against is quoted, with
*edit it by hand, or run `refine`, if it no longer holds*.

## Without the reading map

When the `read` skill, which carries the map, is not installed, the round still runs, in plain
words, and names no work: the explainer and every *Teaches* line drop the sentences that name a
work or point to the map; *Behind it* is never shown, and *show me the paper* is answered with
why; C2 offers only *Learn one idea with me — after your first reading* and *Not now*, with
*Other* for a work of the owner's own, which goes on *Find first* in their words. A retake still
scopes by *Bears on*, which is in this file.
