# Reading map — the evolution of investment research, and where alpha comes from

> **A map of how investment research evolved, and of where alpha comes from.** The researcher
> proposes a work to read only from this file or from the owner's own library, never from memory.
> A work listed here is a **lead, not a source**: nothing is claimed on it until its PDF is in
> `Sources/` and `read` has written its note. Years, authors and titles are given as here, and
> where a title is shortened, given two ways or has no author named, the line says so. A note's
> `citation` comes from the source's own title page, never from this file. The one-line summaries
> are orientation, never evidence; those of *Where alpha comes from* are hints — questions to ask
> of an idea, offered as options — never a position to adopt.

The premise: every belief a person holds about markets has already been written down, tested and
argued with, and research starts by finding out by whom. Investment research did not evolve by
replacement; it evolved by addition.

## How the researcher uses this file

- **Place the belief.** *Where a belief sits* gives, for each stance an owner may hold, the act, the
  arc's question, the work that holds it, who tested it where someone is named, and its *other
  side*. *Beliefs people type* does the same for the beliefs a newcomer states in their own words,
  as `investing-philosophy` places the edge an owner names in its Q4. A belief neither lists is
  placed by the arc question whose works or core ideas name it, in *The six acts*.
- **Name the other side** only as this file names it. Where it says none is named here, say that;
  never supply one.
- **Propose what to find first** from *Start here*, the ten papers of the two timelines, and from
  the belief's own entry. A proposal the owner does not pick is never written.
- **Say where it lands.** A picked work is written after the owner's go, in `RESEARCHER.md` under
  *What you are reading for*: as *Find first* under the question it serves or, when it serves no
  numbered question, on the section's closing *Find first* line, which is added to and never
  rewritten — its *none*, in whatever language it is written, gives way to the first work.
  `interview` writes a work picked from *Suggest a reading* there, and `investing-philosophy` the
  works picked at a round's close. Never download it: the owner finds it by its title and authors,
  puts the PDF in `Sources/Papers/`, or `Sources/Books/` for a book, and runs `read`; a work that
  cannot be found comes off the line by hand.
- **Match before proposing.** Compare each work here with the file names under `Sources/` and the
  notes in `Knowledge/INDEX.md` by the distinctive words of the title, and by the first author's
  surname wherever the file name or the note carries one, ignoring the year, punctuation and
  ellipses: an owner's note may carry another year than this file, and a title may begin with
  ". . ." where this file has "…". A work with a note is *read*, named as held, never offered as
  one to find. A file with no note is *in your Sources/, not yet read*. A title match whose author
  or length disagrees is *possibly in your Sources/*, never *read*: a paper by other authors whose
  title begins *Efficiently Inefficient* is not Pedersen's book of 2015.
- **Offer *Where alpha comes from* as hints.** It holds the five sources of edge, where ideas come
  from and how each fools you, seven questions before any backtest, an idea's anatomy and three
  questions for discussion. Each is offered as a question the owner may answer, edit, skip or
  refuse, never as a position to adopt, and never cited as evidence. `investing-philosophy` teaches
  the five sources after its Q4, on where the owner's edge comes from, and puts *Discussion*, some
  of the seven questions and the kill switch to the owner as questions about how they invest — how
  they would know, how long they would hold, what would make them stop — never as a strategy's
  rules. `objective` takes the anatomy of a claim, and `blueprint` the seven questions and the kill
  switch, for a strategy.

## Start here — the ten papers of the two timelines

### The argument, dated

Five papers, thirty-four years. The map's conclusion, a view to test, not a position to adopt:
read in order, the case for research makes itself.

1. **The claim** — 1970, Fama, *Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work*.
   Prices already reflect what is known; beating the market is luck.
2. **The counterparty** — 1979, Kahneman & Tversky, *Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under
   Risk*. People are not the calculators the claim assumes: losing hurts more than winning feels
   good, and the mistake repeats.
3. **Why it must exist** — 1980, Grossman & Stiglitz, *On the Impossibility of Informationally
   Efficient Markets*. If information costs money, prices cannot be perfect: somebody has to be
   paid to make them right.
4. **Why it persists** — 1997, Shleifer & Vishny, *The Limits of Arbitrage*. Seeing the mispricing
   is not holding the trade; capital, risk and horizon run out first.
5. **Why it keeps moving** — 2004, Lo, *The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis*. Edges get crowded and
   decay, the environment shifts, new ones appear; nothing stays put.

The map's conclusion, a view to test, not a position to adopt: alpha has to exist. It has a
source. It persists. And it keeps moving. Therefore research cannot be a one-time effort: the edge
is in the process that finds the next one, not in any single signal.

### The evidence, dated

Five results, twelve years. Edges are real, they decay, and whether they were ever real is still
being fought over.

6. **Crowding** — 2011, Khandani & Lo, *What Happened to the Quants in August 2007? Evidence from
   Factors and Transactions Data*. August 2007: quant books that shared a strategy unwound together
   in three days; an ecological shock, reconstructed. Question 15 below lists it as 2007, with the
   short title *What Happened to the Quants in August 2007?*, and Act VII cites it as 2007.
7. **Cycles** — 2016, Daniel & Moskowitz, *Momentum Crashes*. Momentum crashes in sharp rebounds: a
   strategy works, gets crowded, breaks, and comes back.
8. **Decay** — 2016, McLean & Pontiff, *Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return
   Predictability?* Anomaly returns fall about 26% out of sample and about 58% once the paper is
   published.
9. **False positives** — 2016, Harvey, Liu & Zhu, *… and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns*.
   Hundreds of factors published; most claimed findings are false once you correct for the number
   of tests. The title is shortened here, from its ellipsis on; search it with the authors' names.
10. **The rebuttal** — 2023, Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, *Is There a Replication Crisis in Finance?*
    Model the factors jointly and 82% replicate, in 13 themes across 93 countries.

The map's conclusion, a view to test, not a position to adopt: real. Decaying. And still
contested. Nobody will settle the factor fight for you: deciding which edges are real,
and for how long, is the job, and it never finishes.

## Where a belief sits

The stances an owner may hold on whether anyone beats the market, where an edge comes from and
whether published edges are real; how the owner likes to decide is not placed here. *Holds it* is
the work the stance comes from; *tested* and *other side* come only from this file, and where no one
is named, the entry says so.

### Can anyone beat the market?

- **No — prices already know.** Holds it: Fama, 1970 (the claim). Act III, question 07, "If alpha
  is rare and fragile, should it be assumed — or must it be proven?" Tested: Cowles, 1933 — put to
  the test, the forecasters were noise; Jensen, 1968 — most active managers fail to generate
  persistent alpha. Other side: Kahneman & Tversky, 1979, the counterparty; Grossman & Stiglitz,
  1980 — perfect efficiency is self-defeating. Inside the frame: a special case — a stable
  environment, heavy competition and slow change.
- **Yes — someone is paid to know.** Holds it: Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980 (why it must exist);
  Pedersen, 2015, *Efficiently Inefficient*. Act III, question 09, "If nobody beats the market…
  why does anyone still pay for research?" Tested: no test is named here. Other side: Berk & Green,
  2004, *Mutual Fund Flows and Performance in Rational Markets* — "alpha exists" is not "the client
  gets it". The map's conclusion, point 01.
- **For a while — edges crowd and move.** Holds it: Lo, 2004 (why it keeps moving). Act V,
  question 14, "So markets are rational… and emotional. What survives both — and for how long?"
  Tested: the evidence timeline — Khandani & Lo, 2011; Daniel & Moskowitz, 2016; McLean & Pontiff,
  2016. Its mechanism: Farmer, 2002. Other side: no paper is set against Lo here. Fama, 1970, is
  the nearest, and the frame makes it a special case, not a rival; the map's own caveat is that the
  frame is hard to falsify, so make it operational.
- **Not sure yet.** No placement. Start at the claim and read the argument in order, 1970 to 2004.

### If there is an edge, where does it come from?

The five sources of edge, in four options: information and implementation share the last.
*Not sure yet* is typed under *Other*.

- **Paid for a risk others avoid.** Risk premia, the first source. Holds it: Ross, 1976. Act III,
  question 08, "If one beta cannot explain returns… how many risks are we being paid to carry?"
  Measured: Fama & French, 1992 and 1993, and Carhart, 1997 — value, size and momentum. Other side:
  Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny, 1994 — the value premium as a mistake, not a risk.
- **A mistake others repeat.** Behavioral, the second source. Holds it: Kahneman & Tversky, 1979
  (the counterparty). Act IV, question 12, "If the anomalies are real… who keeps making the
  mistake?", with Shiller, 1981, and De Bondt & Thaler, 1985. Tested: no test is named here. Other
  side: Fama, 1970, the claim. Why it persists: Shleifer & Vishny, 1997.
- **Others cannot take the other side.** Limits to arbitrage, the third source. Holds it: Shleifer
  & Vishny, 1997 (why it persists). Act IV, question 13, "If mistakes are obvious… why do they
  survive?", with Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny, 1994. Tested: no test is named here. Other side:
  Fama & French, 1992 and 1993 — the value premium as a risk factor, where the same authors call it
  a mistake.
- **Something I see or do better.** Information and implementation, the fourth and fifth sources.
  Place the half the owner's words point to; with nothing to go on, both, a line each.
  *Information*, something measured that the price has not absorbed. Holds it: Grossman & Stiglitz,
  1980 (why it must exist). Act III, question 09, "If nobody beats the market… why does anyone
  still pay for research?" Tested: no test is named here. Other side: Fama, 1970, the claim, which
  this map sets against Grossman & Stiglitz; *Five sources of edge* names Fama with this source too
  — assume the price already knows, and name what it has not absorbed. *Implementation*, the same
  signal with more of it kept after costs. Holds it: Grinold & Kahn, 1995, *Active Portfolio
  Management*. Act VI, question 18, "If an edge is real… how does it become a portfolio — and what
  can a machine add?" Tested: no test is named here. Other side: none is named here.
- **Not sure yet**, typed under *Other*. No placement; Shleifer & Vishny, 1997, is still *why it
  persists* for any edge.

### Are most published findings real?

- **Most are false.** Holds it: Harvey, Liu & Zhu, 2016. Act V, question 17, "If robustness beats
  intuition… how many backtests did it take to find yours?" Other side: Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen,
  2023, the rebuttal.
- **Real, but they shrink once published.** Holds it: McLean & Pontiff, 2016. Act V, question 17:
  anomaly returns roughly halve after publication, because the market reads the papers too. Other
  side: none is named here. Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, 2023, bears on how many were ever real, not
  on how much is left once published.
- **Most hold up when retested together.** Holds it: Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, 2023. Act V,
  question 17. Other side: Harvey, Liu & Zhu, 2016.
- **Harvey, Liu & Zhu and Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen both picked** is the map's own verdict: still
  contested.

## Beliefs people type

Beliefs a newcomer often states in their own words, placed the same way. A belief not listed here
is placed from *The six acts*.

- **What goes up keeps going up — momentum.** Act IV, question 11, "If returns have structure…
  which patterns survive the cost of trading them?" Holds it: Jegadeesh & Titman, 1993, *Returns
  to Buying Winners and Selling Losers* — past winners keep winning for three to twelve months, the
  anomaly that resists a risk explanation best. Tested: Carhart, 1997, as a factor; Asness,
  Moskowitz & Pedersen, 2013, across markets; its crashes, Daniel & Moskowitz, 2016. Other side: no
  work here argues momentum away; its test is decay, McLean & Pontiff, 2016.
- **Cheap stocks beat expensive ones — value.** Act II, question 03, "If prices wander… what is a
  security actually worth, and how much of it should you hold?" Holds it: Graham & Dodd, 1934,
  *Security Analysis*. Why it pays is contested: Fama & French, 1992 and 1993, a risk factor,
  against Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny, 1994, a mistake — extrapolation error. Other side:
  whichever of the two the owner does not hold.
- **Charts and trends show where prices go.** Act I, question 01, "Can prices be predicted at
  all?" Holds it: Dow, 1889, and Nelson, 1903 — trends reflect human behavior. Tested: Cowles,
  1933, *Can Stock Market Forecasters Forecast?* Other side: Cowles, 1933; Kendall, 1953;
  Samuelson, 1965 — price changes look random — and then Lo & MacKinlay, 1988, who rejected the
  random walk on the data.
- **Prices overreact, and bubbles happen.** Act IV, question 12. Holds it: Shiller, 1981, *Do Stock
  Prices Move Too Much?*; De Bondt & Thaler, 1985, *Does the Stock Market Overreact?* Other side:
  Fama, 1970, the claim.
- **Timing the big turns is what matters.** Act V, question 14: regimes matter; Act VII, problem 1,
  regimes you can see coming — Hamilton, 1989, and Ang & Timmermann, 2012, given without titles.
  Other side: Grinold & Kahn, 1995, *Active Portfolio Management* — many small bets beat a few big
  calls; one call on the market is not a strategy.
- **Big players' flows move prices.** Act V, question 15, "If markets adapt… what exactly are they
  adapting to?" Holds it: Farmer, 2002, *Market Force, Ecology and Evolution*; Khandani & Lo, the
  August 2007 unwind. Other side: none is named here; Act VII, problem 2, says the data on the
  crowd is thin, late or proprietary.
- **Machines will find what people miss.** Act VI, question 20, "If clean signals are rare… who
  finds them faster: humans or machines?" Holds it: Gu, Kelly & Xiu, 2020, *Empirical Asset
  Pricing via Machine Learning*. Other side: Act VII, problem 3 — the binding constraint is
  economic structure and sample size, not compute — with Arnott, Harvey & Markowitz, 2019, *A
  Backtesting Protocol in the Era of Machine Learning*.

## The six acts

Each act asks the question the one before could not answer, dated by the works that forced it. The
arc's 21 questions are given as worded here, each followed by its works. *Edge* is each question's
"where the edge is" line, shortened. The edge lines are strategy-stage questions, never questions
for setup: `interview` asks none of them, and `investing-philosophy` may put one to the owner at
Researching only, as a question about how they invest, never as a strategy's rule.

### Act I — Prices: can prices be predicted?

Prices look random; forecasters look like noise.

**01. Can prices be predicted at all?** — Dow, Bachelier & Nelson, *markets as signals*.

- 1889, Dow, *Wall Street Journal editorials* — a body of editorials, not one title.
- 1900, Bachelier, *Théorie de la Spéculation* — the work that changed it.
- 1903, Nelson, *The ABC of Stock Speculation*.
- Edge: if prices carry information, so does their sequence — trend, reversal and volatility
  clustering all start here.

**02. If prices carry information… can anyone read it better than the crowd?** — Cowles, Kendall &
Samuelson, *the first scoreboard*.

- 1933, Cowles, *Can Stock Market Forecasters Forecast?* — the work that changed it.
- 1953, Kendall, *The Analysis of Economic Time-Series, Part I: Prices*.
- 1965, Samuelson, *Proof That Properly Anticipated Prices Fluctuate Randomly*.
- Edge: build the scoreboard first; a strategy not measured against a passive alternative has not
  been tested at all.

### Act II — Value and risk: what is it worth, how much to hold?

Value is a model. Risk is priced.

**03. If prices wander… what is a security actually worth, and how much of it should you hold?** —
Graham, Dodd & Damodaran, *intrinsic value*.

- 1934, Graham & Dodd, *Security Analysis* — the work that changed it.
- 1938, Williams, *The Theory of Investment Value*.
- 1994, Damodaran, *Investment Valuation*.
- Edge: the gap between a story and a number is a hypothesis; mispricing against intrinsic value is
  the oldest edge there is.

**04. If value exists… how do we allocate capital across many bets?** — Markowitz, *risk comes
first*.

- 1952, Markowitz, *Portfolio Selection* — the work that changed it.
- 1959, *Portfolio Selection: Efficient Diversification of Investments* — no author is named on
  the line; it is Markowitz's.
- Edge: in how you combine bets, not only in which ones you pick; correlation is a return you can
  engineer.

**05. If risk can be measured… which risk deserves a reward?** — Sharpe & Lintner, *pricing the
risk*. Treynor and Mossin reached the same result independently; no work of theirs is listed here.

- 1964, Sharpe, *Capital Asset Prices* — the work that changed it.
- 1965, Lintner, *The Valuation of Risk Assets*.
- Edge: before you call it alpha, ask whether it is beta in disguise; strip market exposure first.

### Act III — Efficiency: does anyone beat the market?

Proof is on you — and research is what efficiency pays for.

**06. Does anyone actually beat the market — and if not, why would anyone try?** — Jensen,
*measuring skill*.

- 1968, Jensen, *The Performance of Mutual Funds in the Period 1945–1964* — the work that changed
  it.
- 2004, Berk & Green, *Mutual Fund Flows and Performance in Rational Markets*.
- Edge: set the bar before you look — a risk-adjusted benchmark, not zero. Measure, then believe.

**07. If alpha is rare and fragile, should it be assumed — or must it be proven?** — Fama, *markets
as a discipline*.

- 1970, Fama, *Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work* — the work that
  changed it, also shortened to *Efficient Capital Markets*; timeline paper 1.
- 1991, *Efficient Capital Markets: II* — no author is named on the line; it is Fama's.
- Edge: assume the price already knows; name the information it has not absorbed, or the reason it
  cannot.

**08. If one beta cannot explain returns… how many risks are we being paid to carry?** — Ross,
*risk is multi-dimensional*.

- 1976, Ross, *The Arbitrage Theory of Capital Asset Pricing* — the work that changed it.
- Edge: unnamed risk premia are candidate strategies; find a risk others refuse to hold and get
  paid for holding it.

**09. If nobody beats the market… why does anyone still pay for research?** — Grossman & Stiglitz,
*the paradox that pays for research*.

- 1980, Grossman & Stiglitz, *On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets* — the work
  that changed it; timeline paper 3.
- 2015, Pedersen, *Efficiently Inefficient*.
- Edge: the wage for information others found too expensive to gather; what does it cost to know
  this, and why will the crowd not pay it?

### Act IV — Anomalies: why does the data misbehave?

Returns have structure. Mispricing has a mechanism.

**10. If markets are efficient… why does the evidence refuse to behave?** — Fama, French & Carhart,
*empirical reality*.

- 1992, Fama & French, *The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns*.
- 1993, Fama & French, *Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds* — the work that
  changed it, also given as *Common Risk Factors in Stocks and Bonds*.
- 1997, Carhart, *On Persistence in Mutual Fund Performance*.
- Edge: known factors are the hurdle, not the idea; a new edge survives value, size and momentum
  stripped out.

**11. If returns have structure… which patterns survive the cost of trading them?** — Jegadeesh &
Titman · Amihud, *momentum meets liquidity*.

- 1993, Jegadeesh & Titman, *Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers* — the work that changed
  it.
- 2002, Amihud, *Illiquidity and Stock Returns: Cross-Section and Time-Series Effects*.
- Edge: friction is edge; illiquidity is priced, and the capacity to trade it decides who collects.

**12. If the anomalies are real… who keeps making the mistake?** — Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler &
Shiller, *human behavior*.

- 1979, Kahneman & Tversky, *Prospect Theory* — the work that changed it; timeline paper 2, given
  in full there as *Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk*.
- 1981, Shiller, *Do Stock Prices Move Too Much?*
- 1985, De Bondt & Thaler, *Does the Stock Market Overreact?*
- Edge: systematic human error is the most durable mispricing; which bias makes the counterparty
  sell to you?

**13. If mistakes are obvious… why do they survive?** — Shleifer & Vishny, *limits to arbitrage*.

- 1994, Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny, *Contrarian Investment, Extrapolation, and Risk*.
- 1997, Shleifer & Vishny, *The Limits of Arbitrage* — the work that changed it; timeline paper 4.
- Edge: ask who cannot take the other side — constrained capital, career risk, forced sellers.

### Act V — Discipline: do edges last?

Edges decay and the bar rises.

**14. So markets are rational… and emotional. What survives both — and for how long?** — Lo,
*markets adapt*.

- 1988, Lo & MacKinlay, *Stock Market Prices Do Not Follow Random Walks*.
- 2004, Lo, *The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis* — the work that changed it; timeline paper 5.
- 2017, Lo, *Adaptive Markets: Financial Evolution at the Speed of Thought*.
- Edge: every edge decays; build in the signal that the regime has changed, and be ready to retire
  the strategy.

**15. If markets adapt… what exactly are they adapting to?** — Farmer · Arthur · Soros, *markets
are ecologies*.

- 1987, Soros, *The Alchemy of Finance*.
- 1997, Arthur et al., *Asset Pricing under Endogenous Expectations in an Artificial Stock Market*.
- 2002, Farmer, *Market Force, Ecology and Evolution* — the work that changed it.
- 2007, Khandani & Lo, *What Happened to the Quants in August 2007?* — timeline paper 6, dated
  there 2011 with the long title.
- Edge: map the ecology before you enter it — who else runs this, with how much capital, on what
  horizon.

**16. If alpha decays… what does durable research look like?** — Asness, *research discipline in
practice*.

- 1997, *The Interaction of Value and Momentum Strategies* — no author is named on the line; it is
  Asness's.
- 2013, Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, *Value and Momentum Everywhere* — the work that changed it.
- Edge: combine premia that fail at different times; robustness across markets beats a perfect fit
  in one.

**17. If robustness beats intuition… how many backtests did it take to find yours?** — Harvey, Liu
& Zhu · McLean & Pontiff, *the bar rises*.

- 2016, Harvey, Liu & Zhu, *… and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns* — the work that changed
  it; timeline paper 9.
- 2016, McLean & Pontiff, *Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?* — timeline
  paper 8.
- 2019, Arnott, Harvey & Markowitz, *A Backtesting Protocol in the Era of Machine Learning*.
- How many were ever real is still contested — Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, 2023, timeline paper 10.
- Edge: count your trials; an idea has to be justified before the backtest, which can only fail to
  reject it.

### Act VI — Engineering: how does an idea become a book?

Alpha is residualized, scaled and learned.

**18. If an edge is real… how does it become a portfolio — and what can a machine add?** —
Rosenberg · Grinold & Kahn, *from theory to the desk*.

- 1974, Rosenberg, *Extra-Market Components of Covariance in Security Returns*.
- 1989, Grinold, *The Fundamental Law of Active Management*.
- 1995, Grinold & Kahn, *Active Portfolio Management* — the work that changed it.
- Edge: edge scales with breadth; how many independent bets a year does the idea give you?

**19. If everything has exposure… what is actually yours?** — Paleologo, *from ideas to
portfolios*.

- 2021, Paleologo, *Advanced Portfolio Management* — the work that changed it.
- 2025, *The Elements of Quantitative Investing* — no author is named on the line; it is
  Paleologo's.
- Edge: residualize; what is left after known factors is the only part anyone will pay you for.

**20. If clean signals are rare… who finds them faster: humans or machines?** — Gu, Kelly & Xiu ·
Dixon et al., *machine learning*.

- 2020, Gu, Kelly & Xiu, *Empirical Asset Pricing via Machine Learning* — the work that changed it.
- 2020, Dixon, Halperin & Bilokon, *Machine Learning in Finance: From Theory to Practice*.
- 2023, Kelly & Xiu, *Financial Machine Learning*.
- Edge: let the model learn structure you cannot write down, only where you can say why it should
  exist.

**21. If learning systems can model perception and decision together, how should we design
investment research going forward?** — Guo, Wang, Ni & Shum, *investment systems*.

- 2022, Guo, Wang, Ni & Shum, *Quant 4.0: Engineering Quantitative Investment with Automated,
  Explainable and Knowledge-driven AI* — the work that changed it, also shortened to *Quant 4.0*.
- Edge: the durable edge is the system that keeps generating and retiring hypotheses, not any
  single one of them.

## Nothing is discarded

Inside the Adaptive Markets Hypothesis — Lo, 2004: efficiency depends on the ecology. Each theory
keeps its job; the frame is what lets them stop contradicting each other.

- **Efficient markets** — Fama, 1970. A special case: a stable environment, heavy competition and
  slow change.
- **Behavioral finance** — Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler. Where the maladaptive heuristics come from:
  shortcuts that worked in another environment.
- **Limits to arbitrage** — Shleifer & Vishny, 1997. The mechanism that lets inefficiencies last:
  capital, risk and horizon constraints.
- **Costly information** — Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980. The equilibrium: markets cannot be perfectly
  efficient, or nobody would pay for information.
- **Efficiently inefficient** — Pedersen, 2015. The practical version: just enough inefficiency to
  pay the active managers who compete it away.
- **Market ecology** — Farmer · Santa Fe complexity, 1997–2002. The formal version: strategies as
  species, with interactions you can model.
- **Reflexivity** — Soros, 1987. Feedback between participants and prices, part of what changes the
  environment.

## Why alpha has to exist — the map's conclusion

Kept for the researcher's own orientation, a view to test: never quoted to the owner as a position
to hold.

1. **Alpha has to exist.** Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980 — if information is costly, someone is paid to
   gather it.
2. **It has a source.** Behavioral finance — heuristics that worked in another environment keep
   firing in this one.
3. **It persists.** Limits to arbitrage — capital, risk and horizon constraints on the people who
   see it.
4. **It keeps moving.** Adaptive markets — edges crowd and decay, the environment shifts, new ones
   appear.

Said before anyone else does:

- **The frame is hard to falsify.** So make it operational: measure crowding, capacity, flows and
  the participant mix.
- **Grossman–Stiglitz is the stronger claim.** Use it for why alpha cannot go to zero; use Lo for
  how it moves.
- **Berk & Green cut the other way.** "Alpha exists" is not "the client gets it": capital chases
  skill until the net return is gone. Say which claim you are making.

The edge is in the process, not in any single signal. The research process is discretionary at
design and systematic at scale; scientific principles are what connect the two.

## Act VII is unwritten — four open problems

Each is where the next edge is most likely to come from. They can be picked as questions to read
for; the works named under them are the ones given here, and where a problem names a literature
rather than a work, or a work without its title, the researcher proposes nothing by title.

1. **Regimes you can see coming.** Regimes are named confidently after the fact; forecasting the
   turn in time to act is the difference between surviving a shift and explaining one. Who is on
   it: Hamilton, 1989 · Ang & Timmermann, 2012 · the regime-switching and jump-model literature —
   no titles given.
2. **A census of the crowd.** Who else holds this, with how much capital, on what horizon? The data
   is thin, late or proprietary, so crowding gets measured after it hurts. Who is on it: Khandani &
   Lo, 2007 · flow and positioning research.
3. **Learning that generalizes.** Machine learning fits the past extremely well; the binding
   constraint is economic structure and sample size, not compute. Who is on it: Gu, Kelly & Xiu,
   2020 · Arnott, Harvey & Markowitz, 2019 · Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, 2023.
4. **Research that runs itself.** If edges decay, the asset is the pipeline — data, features, tests
   and attribution, re-runnable on tomorrow's idea. Automate the process, not the signal. Who is on
   it: Guo, Wang, Ni & Shum, 2022 — Quant 4.0 · knowledge-driven AI.

## Where alpha comes from

Each stop on the arc points to a source of edge, and five hold up under scrutiny. Everything here
is a hint: a question to put to an idea, which the owner may answer, edit, skip or refuse, never a
position to adopt. This part gives surnames only; the works behind them are the ones *The six
acts* gives under the question named, and a name the acts give no work for is proposed by no
title.

### Five sources of edge

An idea with no named source of edge is only a pattern: say which source it draws on, and why that
door is still open, before testing anything.

1. **Risk premia** — being paid for carrying a risk others will not. *Ask:* what risk am I
   carrying, and who pays me to carry it? Sharpe · Ross · Fama & French — questions 05, 08 and 10.
2. **Behavioral** — people making the same error, in the same direction, over and over. *Ask:*
   which bias leads the other side to sell to me? Kahneman & Tversky · Thaler · Shiller — question
   12.
3. **Limits to arbitrage** — those who know better are unable to act on it. *Ask:* who cannot take
   the other side, and why? Shleifer & Vishny · Amihud — questions 13 and 11.
4. **Information** — measuring something the price has not yet taken in. *Ask:* what can I see
   that the price cannot, and why am I the one who sees it? Grossman & Stiglitz · Fama · Gu, Kelly &
   Xiu — questions 09, 07 and 20.
5. **Implementation** — the same signal, with more of it kept after costs. *Ask:* can I collect a
   known edge better than others do? Markowitz · Grinold & Kahn · Paleologo — questions 04, 18 and
   19.

`investing-philosophy` names them after its Q4, on where the owner's edge comes from; *Where a
belief sits* places each.

### Where ideas come from, and how each fools you

Six places to look, each with its trap. No work is named here.

1. **Published anomalies** — the factor zoo: hundreds of documented patterns, most with a paper
   behind them. *Trap:* most are noise; prefer an idea with a mechanism that replicated out of
   sample.
2. **Market structure** — index rebalances, option expiries, month-end flows, forced sellers.
   *Trap:* crowded and small; check capacity before getting attached.
3. **Behavioral patterns** — slow reaction to news, overreaction to stories, the disposition
   effect. *Trap:* name the bias and who holds it, or it is only a story.
4. **Frictions** — illiquidity, funding constraints, limits on short sales. *Trap:* the premium
   exists; what is being tested is the cost of collecting it.
5. **Your own data** — something you can measure that others do not, or measure better. *Trap:* a
   new dataset generates hypotheses; it is not one.
6. **Combining known premia** — value with momentum, trend with liquidity, carry with trend.
   *Trap:* the mix is an edge only if its parts fail at different times.

### Seven questions before any backtest

Each with the names set beside it. The test: if the seven answers do not fit in one paragraph, the
idea is not yet a hypothesis.

1. What makes this edge exist? — Grossman & Stiglitz · Fama.
2. Who is on the other side, and what keeps them on the losing end? — Kahneman & Tversky ·
   Shleifer & Vishny.
3. Paid risk or mispricing — and which one am I setting out to own? — Ross · Fama & French.
4. How many independent bets a year does it offer? — Grinold & Kahn.
5. Does it last after costs, and how much capital before it stops working? — Amihud · Paleologo.
6. When does it break, and would I stay in through the break? — Lo · Shiller.
7. How many ideas came before this one? — Harvey, Liu & Zhu · McLean & Pontiff.

### An idea's anatomy

A belief is one sentence; a hypothesis also names its edge, the other side, its test, and what
would make you drop it. Five parts:

- **The claim** — one sentence, in the present tense, about a relationship that can be measured.
- **The source of edge** — which of the five it draws on, and why that door should still be open.
- **The other side** — who sells to you, and what keeps them doing so.
- **The test** — the measurement that settles it, chosen before looking at the data.
- **The kill switch** — the result that ends the idea, rather than sending it back for tuning.

Filled in for a worked idea such as trend in liquid assets, the anatomy shows the shape — a claim a
measurement can settle, a kill switch read off sub-periods net of costs — and is not a claim to
adopt.

### Discussion

Three questions left open, to put to any belief:

- Whose paper does it contradict, and can the owner live with that?
- If alpha decays, how long would the strategy they want to build last?
- What evidence would change their mind before four months go into it?

`investing-philosophy` asks the last two its own way, as questions about the owner rather than a
strategy's rules: how long an edge would last once everybody knows it, and what would change the
owner's mind. The first is the other side it names when it places a belief, offered as a work to
find.
